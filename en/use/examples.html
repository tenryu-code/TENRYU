Examples & presets reference

TENRYU Studio's one-dimensional presets cover basic configurations and configurations that use optional physics kernels. The basic configurations are a pressure-driven shock, a radiative heat wave (Marshak wave), a laser-driven planar foil, a direct-drive shell implosion, and an indirect drive set by a radiation temperature. The configurations with optional physics kernels use SN transport (discrete ordinates), nonlocal electron heat conduction with the Schurtz–Nicolaï–Busquet (SNB) model, cross-beam energy transfer (CBET) with hot electrons, fusion burn, material tables with multigroup radiation, and the cold-equilibrium constitutive law. Every preset was run to its end time on an RTX 4090 (the CBET one on 2026-10-05, the others on 2026-10-01) and checked against a run on a twice finer mesh (to 0.5 ns for the CBET one). Commissioned decks for typical experiments are in examples/laser_plasma_1d/; a separate deck library that reproduces verification items is in examples/verification/. Terms specific to this page are defined in the terms section.

At a glance

  • Every preset was run to its end time and compared with a run on a twice finer mesh (to 0.5 ns for the CBET one); Studio shows the result on each card, under "Verification run" (terms).
  • The meshes are recommended meshes (built from the experimental conditions by tools/assist/assist.py recommend-mesh) for the six laser-driven presets and per-layer tables for the five driven by a radiation temperature or a pressure.
  • Known problems are stated with the results: the outermost boundary cell that keeps accelerating on finer meshes under a spherical Marshak drive, the mesh dependence of the absorbed energy in the SNB foil, the clamping of values in the table foil, and the 0.6 % density difference behind the pressure-driven shock.
  • The canonical regression reference is examples/verification/gxii_1d_fld_regression.py — the GXII benchmark deck (GXII: the twelve-beam GEKKO XII laser at the Institute of Laser Engineering, The University of Osaka).

1D laser-plasma example suite (examples/laser_plasma_1d/)

This suite collects commissioned one-dimensional decks modeling typical laser-plasma experiments.

  • Its ten decks cover foil shock breakout, two-pulse shock timing, impedance matching, an exploding foil, a radiative shock in Kr, a cylindrical liner, solid-sphere convergence (with a 600/900/1350-cell study), a D2-filled shell implosion, a corona with CBET and hot electrons, and Marshak-driven DT burn.
  • The suite README records each deck's status, measured commissioning metrics, and caveats.
  • The solid materials use tmat equation-of-state (EOS) tables that are not distributed because they derive from licensed PrOpacEOS data (PrOpacEOS is a code that builds EOS and opacity tables). Users must generate them with tools/tmat/propaceos_to_tmat.py from their own PrOpacEOS/SESAME workflow; one deck (ex06) additionally requires a SESAME ASCII library (SESAME is in the glossary).

Terms used in this calculation

The presets and the example suite use words with meanings specific to TENRYU's implementation for the way meshes are made, the treatment of cold matter before the laser arrives, implosion metrics, and material tables. They are defined here together.

Commissioned decks

A commissioned deck is a deck whose metrics, measured in actual runs, are recorded in a README. Commissioning means operating a device or a setup and checking that it behaves as designed. The ten decks of examples/laser_plasma_1d/ were generated and tuned with the deck generation of tools/assist (generate-deck) and then run one by one against target ranges of metrics fixed at the design stage (the README calls them design windows; an example is the allowed range of the time at which the shock reaches the rear face). For each deck, the README table gives whether it completed, the measured metrics, and where they stand relative to the target ranges.

Metrics that fall outside a target range are recorded too. For example, the stagnation time of ex08 is 5.03 ns against a target range of 3.6–4.5 ns, which is late. Being commissioned therefore does not mean that every metric lies inside its target range. With the record, the change in the results after you modify a deck can be read against the measured values of the original deck. When adapting a deck to your conditions, read the README's measured values and caveats first and check which metrics match the design intent.

Material tables (tmat)

tmat is the value of model that selects TENRYU's material table in the TMAT-H5 format (TMAT-H5). The HDF5 file (extension .tmat.h5) holds the equation of state (EOS) and the multigroup opacity as tables. A deck points to a table from the material's eos=dict(model="tmat", file="TMAT-H5/<material>.tmat.h5") and opacity=dict(model="tmat", file=…). Unlike the ideal-gas EOS (model="ideal_gas") and the constant opacity (model="constant"), a tmat table gives, by interpolation, the pressure and the energy as functions of density and temperature and the opacity in each photon-energy group (EOS, opacity, and ionization).

Among Studio's presets, table_radiation_foil uses the EOS and the 80-group opacity of TMAT-H5/CD.tmat.h5, and cold_equilibrium_layers uses the EOS of TMAT-H5/CD.tmat.h5 and TMAT-H5/D2.tmat.h5 (with a constant opacity). The ten decks of the example suite use a tmat EOS table for their solid materials. The tmat tables derive from licensed PrOpacEOS data and are therefore not distributed; users build them from their own PrOpacEOS or SESAME workflow with tools/tmat/propaceos_to_tmat.py.

Recommended meshes and per-layer tables

A recommended mesh is an initial mesh that is built deterministically from the experimental conditions for a laser-driven 1D deck. tools/assist/assist.py recommend-mesh reads the laser wavelength, the pulse shape and intensity, the target layers (material, atomic mass and number, density, thickness), the geometry, and the end time. From a reference table of mesh-convergence calculations (tools/assist/data/mesh_convergence_reference.json), it fixes an upper limit on the cell mass of the surface cell divided by the area of the target's outer surface (the areal density, in g/cm²) and writes out the Mesh block. It uses no LLM. In laser-driven calculations, the size of the surface cell decides the absorption and ablation results, which is why this limit is needed.

The deck of a recommended mesh contains Mesh.zoning_intent, which declares the cell layout, and Mesh.resolution_requirement, which imposes the limit on the solver (apply="enforce", and empirical, which records the measurements the recommendation rests on). When the server's tenryu is available, the tool rebuilds the mesh until it passes tenryu validate and recommends only a mesh that passed. A recommendation is made for conditions within the range that the reference table measured. Outside the range it uses a calibrated a priori limit and flags the result extrapolation; in that case, make a deck whose surface cell mass is halved and compare it with the original deck to check the convergence. Among Studio's presets, the six laser-driven ones (laser_foil, laser_shell_implosion, snb_nonlocal_foil, cbet_hot_electrons, table_radiation_foil, and cold_equilibrium_layers) store a recommended mesh made for their conditions (the operation is described on the TENRYU Studio page).

A per-layer table is a mesh method in which the number of cells and the spacing are set for each layer (a material region). The spacing is equal width, equal mass, or geometric; for a geometric spacing, the width of the end cell or the width ratio of adjacent cells is given. Studio computes the nodes and writes them into the deck as Mesh.explicit_nodes, so the grid displayed is exactly the one used in the calculation. A recommended mesh takes its limit from the laser conditions, so it can be made only for a laser-driven deck. The five presets driven by a radiation temperature or a pressure (pressure_driven_shock, marshak_wave, indirect_drive_capsule, sn_radiation_foil, and burn_exploding_pusher) use a per-layer table: the finest cells are on the side where the drive enters, the cell widths are continuous across material interfaces, and the convergence was checked by comparison with a run on a twice finer mesh.

Mesh difference and verification run

The mesh difference is the difference (in %), observable by observable, between the result of the same setup run on a twice finer mesh and the result of the preset. The twice finer mesh has twice the number of cells and half the mass in every cell (the surface cell included); if the observables do not change when the mesh is refined, they are converged on the preset's mesh. The convergence target for the absorbed energy is 3 %. Differences that exceed the target, such as the 7.0 % of the absorbed energy in the SNB preset, are stated as they are.

The verification run is the name of the result field on a Studio card: the run of the preset to its end time in which the mesh difference was measured. It is distinct from tenryu validate, which checks an input, and from tenryu verify, which runs the solver's verification. Studio's "Convergence pair" builds the same finer deck for your own deck (the case name ends in _fine) so that you can make the same check (see the TENRYU Studio page).

The corona ramp, the ghost corona, and radial absorption

When a cold solid surface meets the vacuum through a one-cell density jump, 351 nm light reflects at the critical surface (the surface where the electron density reaches the critical density) with almost no path along which it can be absorbed, and no seed exists for a corona grown by heating (1D mesh). To avoid this, either a corona ramp or a ghost corona is placed. The laser absorption in 1D also has two modes: raytrace_2d, which traces rays, and the simple radial absorption. The three terms below are defined to tell which of them each laser-driven preset of this page uses.

The corona ramp is a layer in which the density falls exponentially outward from the outer surface of the target; it is placed as part of the initial density profile. The cells of the layer are fluid cells that carry mass and move with the other cells. The solver has no key for this layer; Studio's form "Pre-plasma corona ramp" writes it into the deck's density and volume-fraction functions. It is disabled by default and the presets do not use it (1D mesh).

The ghost corona (Laser.lasermesh.ghost_corona) is a virtual low-density layer that is placed on the laser mesh (the radial grid on which the rays are traced), for the ray tracing only; it leaves the fluid density, temperature, and mass unchanged (1D only). The layer lies outside the outermost material cell. Its electron density falls exponentially outward from the value of the outermost material cell (capped at ne_max_frac times the critical density \(n_c\)) and ends at ne_min_frac times \(n_c\). Its width is the larger of n_out fine cells of the laser mesh and the sound speed times the time since the laser turned on, and it shrinks and vanishes as the corona becomes resolved on the fluid side. The power absorbed in the ghost corona is redistributed to a few cells inward from the outermost material cell (docs/NUMERICS.md §5.7.5 in the repository). The default of n_out is 12, of ne_min_frac 0.03, and of ne_max_frac 0.99, the same for a deck that does not set them and for Studio. The default of enabled is off both for a deck that does not set it and for Studio; Studio's laser-driven presets turn it on (the difference from the corona ramp).

Radial absorption (Laser.mode="radial_absorption_1d") is a simple laser-absorption mode, for 1D only, that does not trace rays. It attenuates the sum of the power of all beams as one radial flux going from the outside inward, cell by cell, by inverse bremsstrahlung, stops it where the electron density reaches the critical density, and books the remainder as unabsorbed. The beam direction, F-number, focus, intensity profile, and number of rays do not change the absorption profile (2D RZ and the simple radial mode). The other mode, raytrace_2d, traces rays. Among Studio's presets, the four planar foils (laser_foil, snb_nonlocal_foil, table_radiation_foil, and cold_equilibrium_layers) use radial absorption and the two shells (laser_shell_implosion and cbet_hot_electrons) use raytrace_2d. All six presets use the ghost corona.

The cold-equilibrium constitutive law

The cold-equilibrium constitutive law adds a correction to a tabulated equation of state in cold states of low electron temperature and puts a specified reference state in mechanical equilibrium. At very low temperature (cold solids and liquids), a tabulated equation of state can deviate from the total pressure of 0 that matter without external force has (a flat cold curve, tension, spinodal regions; docs/NUMERICS.md §1 (b) in the repository). Used as it is, the pressure difference between layers moves an interface before the shock arrives. The law sets the total pressure of the reference state \((\rho_0,T_{e0},T_{i0})\) to \(P_0\) and its bulk modulus to \(K_0\), and takes the pressure of cold matter to be

\[ P_{\rm cold}(v)=P_0+K_0\left(\frac{v_0}{v}-1\right) \]

where \(v=1/\rho\) is the specific volume and \(v_0=1/\rho_0\). A density-dependent correction is added to the electron free energy so that, at the reference temperatures and near the reference density, the total pressure equals \(P_{\rm cold}\).

The correction has its full size for an electron temperature \(T_e\) at or below transition_begin_fraction (default 0.5) times T_start_eV, and vanishes at or above T_start_eV, with a smooth connection (continuous up to the second derivative) in between. Writing T_start_eV as \(T_*\), the parts heated to \(T_*\) or above return to the result of the original table. In density, the correction is applied in full between 1/1.10 and 1.10 times the reference density (density_core_ratio) and tapers to 0 at 1/1.50 and 1.50 times (density_outer_ratio). Cold cells are not fixed as a rigid wall: every cell responds mechanically, so a shock propagates through the cold layers.

In a deck, Numerics.hydro.T_start_inactive_cells="cold_equilibrium" (default "passive_fill") turns the law on, and Numerics.hydro.T_start_eV (it must be greater than 0; default 0) gives \(T_*\). Each material carries eos=dict(…, cold_reference=dict(rho_gcc, Te0_eV, Ti0_eV, P0_dyn_cm2, bulk_modulus_dyn_cm2)) (the default of P0_dyn_cm2 is 0; the others are required). The conditions are 1D_SPH, two temperatures, a table EOS for every non-void material, and Numerics.hydro.qei_heat_capacity="table" (the electron-ion exchange uses the tabulated heat capacities).

The four keys of Numerics.hydro.cold_equilibrium that shape the correction (transition_begin_fraction 0.5, density_core_ratio 1.10, density_outer_ratio 1.50, and inverse_max_iterations 80) have the same defaults for a deck that does not set them and in Studio. When the law is selected, Studio sets the heat capacity of the exchange to "table" and fills each material's reference density and temperatures from the initial state of the material's first region (the total pressure is 0 and the bulk modulus is left empty). cold_equilibrium_layers uses T_start_eV = 2 eV and the reference states of liquid D2 (0.17 g/cm³, 0.1 eV, bulk modulus 0.12 GPa) and CD (1.05 g/cm³, 0.1 eV, 5.8 GPa); it shows that the interface between the two layers does not move until the shock arrives and how the shock propagates (see the TENRYU Studio page).

Stagnation, bang time, and convergence ratio

Stagnation is the period in which an imploding shell stops its inward motion, with the shell radius at its minimum and the density at its maximum. TENRYU records the peak density \(\rho_{\rm peak}\) as implosion/rho_peak and the mean shell radius as implosion/shell_radius_mean in the history at every step. In 1D, the shell is the set of cells whose density is at least 0.1 times \(\rho_{\rm peak}\), and the mean radius is the mass-weighted average of the radii of the centers of those cells. The stagnation peak is short (about 15 ps in a GXII-class laser implosion computed on 2026-07-31), and sparse snapshots underestimate the maximum density, so values and times are read from the history (Outputs and analysis).

The bang time is the time at which the nuclear reaction rate peaks in an implosion with burn. A deck with burn writes the cumulative number of DT neutrons to burn/N_neutrons_dt_cum in the history; the reaction rate is the time derivative of this cumulative number. The bang_time_s of the golden (the stored reference result) on the verification page is a name for the time at which implosion/shell_radius_min is at its minimum (the time of maximum compression) in the GXII deck, which has no burn (src/drivers/cmd_verify.cpp); it is not a bang time in this sense.

The convergence ratio (CR) measures how much an implosion shrinks the radius: the initial shell radius \(R_{\rm initial}\) divided by the shell radius \(R_{\rm shell}\) at the time.

\[ \mathrm{CR}=\frac{R_{\rm initial}}{R_{\rm shell}},\qquad R_{\rm shell}=\frac{\sum_{c}m_c\,r_c}{\sum_{c}m_c}\quad(\rho_c\ge0.1\,\rho_{\rm peak}) \]

Here \(m_c\), \(r_c\), and \(\rho_c\) are the mass, the radius of the center, and the density of cell \(c\), and the sums run over the cells whose density is at least 0.1 times \(\rho_{\rm peak}\). \(R_{\rm initial}\) is \(R_{\rm shell}\) at the first valid step of the run (src/diagnostics/diagnostics.cu). When Numerics.diagnostics.icf is enabled, the value at each step goes to diagnostics/icf/v1/CR in the history. The minimum radius in the table below is the minimum of \(R_{\rm shell}\), and the convergence ratio is \(R_{\rm initial}\) divided by that minimum radius. For laser_shell_implosion, for example, \(R_{\rm initial}\) of the initial shell (radius 243–250 µm) is about 247 µm, and dividing it by the minimum radius of 49 µm gives 5.0.

Reading the results

The pressure-driven shock is the model of a basic check: measure the shock speed from the shock position over time and compare it, with the density and the flow velocity behind the shock, against the exact Rankine–Hugoniot values. In the radiative heat wave, look at the whole temperature profile, not only at the wall; the front position does not survive a change of the density, the opacity or the heat capacity.

In the laser-driven presets, read the absorbed fraction from the ratio of the deposited to the incident laser energy (the history entries energy/laser_deposited and energy/laser_incident are both cumulative over the run), and the ablation pressure as its mean until the shock reaches the rear wall. The rear face of a planar foil is a rigid wall, so the compression after the shock reaches it comes from the reflected shock; it is not an implosion's stagnation. In the spherical implosions, read the peak density and the stagnation time from per-step history metrics such as implosion/rho_peak: the peak lasts briefly, and the maximum over sparse snapshots misses it. With a cold solid surface alone — no ghost corona and no corona ramp — the laser (the rays in a ray trace, the radial flux in radial absorption) has almost no path along which it is absorbed and reflects at the critical surface, so the drive does not form. Studio's laser-driven presets use the ghost corona.

Whether a result depends on the mesh is checked by running the same setup on a twice finer mesh (the "Convergence pair" in the Geometry tab of Studio); the mesh differences above are the result of that comparison. The cases whose difference exceeds the target, and the defects that appear only on the finer mesh, are stated in the table and on the Studio cards.

Absorption figures and their different conditions

This documentation quotes several laser-absorption numbers that are not comparable with one another, because they belong to different irradiation geometries and opacity models:

  • ≈96% — the GXII golden-regression deck (golden of 2026-09-29): the golden's absorbed energy of 9.53 kJ divided by the incident energy of the deck's pulse, 9.9 kJ (a trapezoidal pulse of 1×1013 W with 10 ps rise and fall, 1 ns wide). Twelve f/3 beams, each a fourth-order super-Gaussian spot of 200 µm radius (where the intensity falls to e−2), on a target of radius 250 µm: a broad drive, with constant-opacity ideal-gas benchmark physics.
  • 89% and 67% — the Studio planar-foil preset (planar irradiation at 351 nm, 1e14 W/cm²) and the shell-implosion preset (a 527 nm Gaussian pulse on a CD shell of 250 µm outer radius), both from the runs of 2026-10-01, with wavelengths, intensities and targets that differ from the regression deck's.
  • ≈45% — the cross-code benchmark against HELIOS (a commercial 1D Lagrangian radiation-hydrodynamics code from Prism Computational Sciences; LTE (local thermodynamic equilibrium), matched irradiation; measured in July 2026): a deliberately narrow ≈113 µm Gaussian spot from a nearly collimated f/191 beam, with LTE table opacities (see Verification & benchmarks).

Absorbed fraction depends strongly on the wavelength, the intensity, the spot size, the beam aperture and the opacity model, so these numbers are not expected to agree, and their disagreement carries no significance.

Verification deck library

examples/verification/ is the deck library for reproducing analytic, conservation, single-operator, and integrated regression checks; this page does not enumerate every case. The canonical practical 1D reference spanning direct laser drive, two-temperature matter, hydrodynamics, conduction, and multigroup FLD is gxii_1d_fld_regression.py. The deck defines the golden-regression input, and its comments record why the initial corona ramp is needed. When developing a laser deck, consult those comments together with the pulse, material interfaces, and output history rather than copying key names alone.

Presets are starting points for learning and for building new decks. The verification decks exist to reproduce the results that analytic solutions and conservation laws require and to detect changes in results. A plausible preset metric and a passing check are complementary evidence, and neither substitutes for the other.
